      #85 Why "Trip Setpoint" ≠ "Safe Operating Limit" And how misunderstanding this breaks IEC 61511 compliance One of the most common friction points on continuous process capital projects happens during the handover from Process Safety HAZOP/LOPA to the Safety Requirements Specification. It comes down to confusing three distinct boundaries: 1. SOL (Safe Operating Limit): the physical limit beyond which a hazardous event occurs. Vessel design pressure, runaway reaction temperature threshold. 2. TSP (Trip Setpoint): the engineered value configured in the SIS logic solver where the safety function is commanded to act. 3. MOL (Maximum Operating Limit): the normal process boundary the BPCS is expected to hold. You cannot set Trip Setpoint = Safe Operating Limit. The SIF has to act early enough to stop the excursion before it reaches the SOL which means the setpoint has to account for real dynamic response time and measurement uncertainty. Three audit failures I keep seeing under IEC 61511 Edition 2: 👉 Setting a high high trip near 95% of a transmitter's calibrated span, right where non-linearity spikes. 👉 Setting the BPCS high alarm too close to the SIS trip point ISA-18.2 territory so the operator gets flooded right when the safety system is about to act anyway. 👉 Operations quietly lowering a trip setpoint to kill nuisance trips, without ever re-verifying whether the process dynamic response rate shifted. The margin shrinks and nobody notices. ***** References for anyone who wants to go deeper: IEC 61511-1:2016 Clause 10.3, ANSI/ISA-18.2, CCPS Guidelines for Safe Automation of Chemical Processes.       The Fourth Layer of LOPA: Safety Instrumented System (SIS) What happens if the BPCS cannot control the process and operator intervention is no longer enough? This is when the Safety Instrumented System (SIS) steps in—the fourth layer of protection in Layers of Protection Analysis (LOPA). Unlike the Basic Process Control System (BPCS), which is designed to keep the process running normally, the SIS is designed to bring the plant to a safe state when hazardous conditions are detected. It is an Independent Protection Layer (IPL), operating separately from the BPCS to ensure reliable action during critical situations. A typical SIS consists of three essential components: • Sensors that detect dangerous conditions such as high pressure, high temperature, or low level. • A Logic Solver (Safety PLC) that evaluates the signals and determines whether a safety action is required. • Final Elements—such as shutdown valves, emergency isolation valves, or motor trips—that execute the protective action. Consider a Naphtha Hydrotreater (NHT) reactor. If reactor pressure continues to rise despite the actions of the BPCS and the operator, the pressure eventually reaches the High-High Pressure trip setpoint. At that moment, the SIS automatically initiates an Emergency Shutdown (ESD) by isolating the feed, tripping the charge pump, shutting down the heater if necessary, and placing the reactor in a safe condition before equipment failure can occur. The SIS is not designed to improve production or optimize the process. Its sole mission is to protect people, assets, and the environment by preventing a process deviation from becoming a major accident.

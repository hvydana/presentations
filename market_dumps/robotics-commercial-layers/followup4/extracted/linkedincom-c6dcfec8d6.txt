AI for Robotics — Foundation Models Meet the Physical World
Issue #42:
How vision-language-action models extend the multimodal architecture from Issue #20 into physical control, why the sim-to-real gap remains one of robotics' hardest unsolved problems, and an honest assessment of what embodied AI can and cannot do today. The Arc 7 finale.
👋 Welcome back to From First Principles.
This is Issue #42 — the Arc 7 finale.
We've covered AI making genuine progress on some of the hardest problems in biology, chemistry, materials science, mathematics, and atmospheric physics. Each domain shared a common structural ingredient: a clearly defined, checkable notion of what "correct" looks like. Protein structures either match crystallography or they don't. Crystal stability either satisfies DFT or it doesn't. Weather forecasts either match historical observation or they don't.
Robotics is different. And that difference is instructive.
In robotics, the AI doesn't just predict something about the world — it has to act in the world, observe what happened, and adapt. The gap between generating a correct answer in a computational domain and successfully grasping a coffee cup off a table in a physical environment turns out to be one of the deepest challenges in AI — not because of any single technical limitation, but because of a cascade of interdependent hard problems that each individually resist the verifiable-correctness pattern that powered progress in every prior issue in this arc.
This issue explains the architecture of modern robotic AI, why the sim-to-real gap remains so hard, what the most promising current approaches look like, and an honest assessment of where embodied AI actually stands in 2026.

This perceptual problem is substantially harder than the vision problems addressed in Issue #20's multimodal models, because it requires estimating physical properties, not just recognizing semantic content.
The action problem
Deciding what to do given a perception isn't enough — the action has to be physically executed. Robot control involves:
Kinematics and dynamics: computing joint trajectories that move the robot's end-effector (hand) to the right position and orientation, respecting the robot's physical constraints and avoiding collisions.
Contact modeling: grasping objects requires precise contact — the fingers need to apply the right forces at the right positions on the object's surface to hold it without dropping it or crushing it. Contact mechanics are notoriously hard to model precisely, because they depend on surface geometry, material properties, and friction coefficients at the point of contact in ways that are difficult to measure or predict with high fidelity.
Real-time control: unlike language generation (where latency of a second is often fine), robot control loops typically run at 100–1000 Hz — every 1–10 milliseconds, the controller needs to update joint commands. This speed requirement places severe constraints on the computational approaches that can realistically run in a robot's control loop.
The physical world is unstructured and unpredictable
A language model's world — text — is fully described by the tokens it receives. A robot's world is open-ended and continuously changing in ways that no sensor fully captures: lighting changes, objects get moved, surfaces are wet or worn, tools break, unexpected obstacles appear.
This means robotic systems need to be robust to the full distribution of real-world conditions, not just the conditions present during training — exactly the distribution shift problem that Issues #21 and #28 discussed in the context of language models, here with physical consequences if the system fails (a dropped object, a collision, a failed grasp) rather than just a wrong text output.
Foundation models for robotics — the new architectural paradigm
The approach that has dominated robotics AI research since roughly 2022 is applying the same large-scale pretrained foundation model paradigm (Issue #1–5) to robotic control — training on large, diverse robot demonstration data to learn general manipulation capabilities, then fine-tuning for specific tasks.
RT-2 — vision-language-action models
RT-2 (Robotics Transformer 2, Google DeepMind, 2023) is the clearest example of directly extending a multimodal vision-language model (Issue #20's architecture) into robotic action.
The key extension: add robot actions as a third modality alongside vision and language. Represent robot actions (joint positions, end-effector poses, gripper commands) as tokens — directly tokenizing continuous action vectors into a discrete vocabulary — and train the model to predict the next action token the same way a language model predicts the next word token.
This seemingly simple extension has a dramatic implication: all the world knowledge, visual understanding, and language reasoning capability of a large vision-language model becomes directly accessible to the robot's decision-making process, rather than being separated from the robot's action policy by a translation layer.
Example of what this enables: a robot running RT-2 can follow instructions like "move the banana to the green cloth" (requiring visual grounding) and "pick up the thing that indicates bad weather" (requiring general world knowledge — inferring that an umbrella indicates rain, not from training on robot-umbrella demonstrations, but from the pretrained model's world knowledge), without being specifically trained on demonstrations for those exact instruction phrasings or visual configurations.
The key architectural connection to Issue #20: RT-2 uses a VLM (Vision-Language Model) backbone (specifically, a version of PaLI-X) and adds action tokens to its output vocabulary — the same patch-based visual encoding, the same transformer processing of mixed visual-text sequences, now extended to also predict robot joint commands. The architecture is genuinely continuous with everything in the multimodal models discussion.
OpenVLA and open-source alternatives
RT-2 was a Google DeepMind system with limited external access. OpenVLA (Open Vision-Language-Action model) is an open-source analogue — a 7B parameter VLA model trained on the Open X-Embodiment dataset (a large collaborative collection of robot demonstration data from multiple research groups and robot platforms) using a similar architectural approach.
OpenVLA and related open-source VLA models have made foundation-model-based robotics research substantially more accessible outside major industry labs, enabling the research community to study, fine-tune, and extend these approaches in ways that proprietary systems prevent.
The data problem for robotics
Foundation models for language and vision were enabled by enormous datasets of human-generated text and images existing on the internet. Robot demonstration data doesn't exist at comparable scale — there's no internet of robots.
Collecting robot demonstration data is slow and expensive: a human demonstrator physically guides a robot through desired motions, one demonstration at a time. This is the opposite of the web-scale data collection that enabled large language models.
The Open X-Embodiment dataset (compiled collaboratively by over 30 research institutions) represents a serious attempt to aggregate robot demonstration data at a useful scale — but even this large collaborative effort produced roughly 1 million demonstrations across multiple robot platforms, far smaller than the datasets that powered transformative progress in language and vision.
Synthetic data from simulation (directly connecting to Issue #23's synthetic data discussion) is a key strategy for addressing this gap — generate robot demonstration data in physics simulation, where it's cheap and unlimited, then transfer to the real world. Which brings us directly to the hardest problem in all of modern robotics.
The sim-to-real gap — why it's so hard
Simulation is used pervasively in robotics research: a physics simulator provides a virtual environment where a robot can interact with objects, receive reward signals, and be trained at a speed and scale that physical experiments cannot match (a simulated robot can train for thousands of "hours" overnight; a physical robot can train for actual hours per overnight session).
The sim-to-real gap is the systematic difference between simulated environments and the real physical world — a difference that causes policies trained in simulation to fail when deployed on real hardware, even when the simulation appears realistic.
Why simulation is always wrong, specifically
Contact and friction: simulating the precise physics of object contact — how surfaces deform, how friction varies with speed and normal force, how objects rotate when grasped — requires detailed knowledge of material properties that are almost never precisely measured, and simulation approximations of these properties can produce qualitatively different behavior than the real physics.
Visual domain gap: simulated images look different from real camera images in ways that are subtle but systematic — lighting, surface texture, sensor noise, depth ambiguity. A policy that learned to recognize objects in simulation may fail to recognize them in reality because the visual appearance differs in ways the policy hasn't been trained to be robust to.
Rigid body assumptions: most physics simulators model objects as perfectly rigid bodies. Real objects deform (soft manipulation tasks — handling fabric, deformable food, cables — are notoriously hard for simulation-based training).
Unmodeled dynamics: real robots have motor friction, joint elasticity, backlash, and sensor noise that vary across units and change over time with wear — properties that simulators typically either ignore or model imprecisely.
Approaches to bridging the gap
Domain randomization: during training, randomly vary simulation parameters — object masses, friction coefficients, visual appearances, lighting conditions, camera positions — across a wide range, so the trained policy learns to be robust to variability rather than specifically optimized for any single simulation configuration. The idea: if the real world is somewhere in the distribution of randomized simulations, a policy robust to that full distribution should transfer.
Sim-to-real adaptation: techniques that explicitly train the policy to adapt from simulation to reality, including real-data fine-tuning (using a small amount of expensive real robot experience to adapt a simulation-trained policy) and domain adaptation methods borrowed from the transfer learning literature.
Real-world data at scale: the most reliable solution is simply using more real-world robot demonstration data — which is why the Open X-Embodiment dataset effort and commercial robot fleets (companies like Figure AI, 1X Technologies, Physical Intelligence, and Boston Dynamics all collecting proprietary real-world data at scale) are significant. Nothing fully replaces actual real-world experience.
Physical Intelligence (π0) — the 2024 frontier
Physical Intelligence (π, founded 2023 by former Google Brain and Stanford robotics researchers) published π0 (pi-zero) in late 2024 — arguably the most capable and well-evaluated general-purpose manipulation policy demonstrated to date.
The key architectural choice: π0 uses a flow matching approach (related to but distinct from diffusion models from Issues #20/#35) for action generation rather than discrete tokenization — generating smooth, continuous action trajectories rather than discretized action tokens. This produces qualitatively more dexterous, fluent manipulation than token-based action generation, which tends to produce slightly jerky motion from the discretization.

Training approach: pre-trained on a large proprietary dataset of diverse robot demonstrations across multiple robot platforms, then fine-tuned for specific tasks. The pre-training provides general manipulation priors (how to handle objects, how to navigate contact, how to coordinate arm and hand motion) that make task-specific fine-tuning require far fewer demonstrations than training from scratch.
Demonstrated capabilities: π0 showed impressive performance on dexterous manipulation tasks including laundry folding, assembling boxes, and bus tub clearing — tasks that require handling deformable objects and sequencing multiple manipulation steps, traditionally among the hardest problems in robot manipulation. These demonstrations were conducted on real hardware, not just simulation.
The honest scope: these are demonstrations in controlled lab conditions, with specific objects and specific task configurations, not general-purpose manipulation across the full diversity of real-world environments. The gap between "impressive lab demonstration" and "deployed in an uncontrolled real-world environment" remains substantial — not because the technology is fundamentally blocked, but because the engineering and data requirements for broad real-world generalization are substantial and ongoing.
The embodiment gap — what all of this still doesn't solve
Even with the most capable current foundation-model-based robotic systems, a fundamental gap remains between AI's capabilities in language and vision and AI's capabilities in physical embodiment.
Language and vision generalize from training data remarkably well. A language model trained on text from the internet generalizes to novel prompts, topics, and phrasings that were never explicitly in the training data, because the underlying statistical patterns generalize. A vision model generalizes to novel images because visual features transfer across contexts.
Physical manipulation generalizes much more narrowly. A robot policy trained to pour liquid from one specific cup into one specific bowl in one specific orientation on one specific table surface may fail when any of those specifics changes — a different cup shape, a slightly different lighting condition, the bowl positioned slightly differently. The generalization gap in physical manipulation is qualitatively wider than in language or vision, because the physical world introduces continuous, high-dimensional variation that doesn't reduce to the same kinds of statistical patterns that text and images do.
Why this gap exists mechanistically: the generalization in language models comes partly from the enormous scale and diversity of training data (essentially all human text ever digitized) and partly from the fact that language is a relatively low-dimensional, structured space. Physical manipulation involves a much higher-dimensional interaction space, with training data that is orders of magnitude smaller, on tasks where the relevant variation (contact geometry, friction, object mass) is not directly observable from the demonstrations the model learns from.
What's genuinely working in physical robotic AI — the honest 2026 picture
Structured industrial environments: robot arms in highly controlled manufacturing environments (welding, precise assembly with engineered fixtures, pick-and-place in structured warehouses) have been reliably automated for decades, predating foundation models. This category is "solved" in the engineering sense — commercial products are in production at scale worldwide.
Warehouse automation: mobile robots navigating structured warehouse environments and locating specific shelves is a deployed commercial technology (Amazon Robotics, Ocado) that works at scale — though this relies on structured environments designed with the robot in mind, not the general unstructured world.
Foundation-model-assisted dexterous manipulation in lab conditions: the RT-2 and π0 class of results are real and meaningful — robots performing general instruction-following and dexterous manipulation tasks that would have been impossible with prior approaches. These results represent genuine capability improvements over the non-foundation-model baselines they replaced.
The deployment gap: the honest current situation is that the most impressive foundation-model-based robotic capabilities are demonstrated in research lab settings, with ongoing commercial deployment efforts by several well-funded companies (Physical Intelligence, Figure AI, 1X, Agility Robotics for humanoids; Covariant, Machina Labs for industrial manipulation). Full-scale deployment into uncontrolled real-world environments — the kind of general-purpose household robot that handles truly open-ended tasks — remains unsolved as of 2026, though the trajectory of progress is faster than at any prior point in the field's history.
The verifiable-correctness pattern — and why robotics doesn't fully fit it
This is the appropriate place to close the arc's intellectual thread.
The pattern that explained progress across Issues #37–41: AI progress concentrates wherever there is a clear, mechanically checkable notion of correctness — protein structures verified against crystallography, crystal stability verified by DFT, weather forecasts verified against historical observation, mathematical constructions verified by executing the program.
Robotics partially but incompletely fits this pattern. Whether a robot successfully grasped an object is checkable (the object is in the gripper or it isn't). Whether a task was completed is often checkable (the cup is on the shelf or it isn't). In this sense, robot learning with dense reward signals (does the robot succeed at the specific task) is a verifiable-reward problem, and it drives progress on specific tasks in specific environments.
The failure of this pattern to fully apply to general robotics: the verification is only meaningful within the specific environment and task configuration where it was obtained. Unlike DFT calculations, which tell you something about thermodynamic stability that generalizes across environments, a robot successfully grasping this specific cup at this specific position tells you much less about its ability to grasp that slightly different cup at a slightly different position. The verification is local and specific in a way that prevents it from driving the kind of broad generalization that fueled the other arc issues.
This is, ultimately, why robotics is the hardest problem in this arc — and why the honest answer to "where is embodied AI in 2026?" is "making rapid, genuine progress on specific tasks in specific environments, with the challenge of broad real-world generalization remaining the central open problem."
3 practical takeaways
1. Foundation model robotics represents a genuine architectural shift — not just incremental improvement. The ability to leverage pretrained world knowledge, visual understanding, and language instructions directly in a robot's action policy (as RT-2 and π0 demonstrate) is qualitatively different from prior robotics approaches, and early evidence suggests it produces qualitatively better generalization within the training distribution. This is the right architectural direction; the open question is how far it scales.
2. The sim-to-real gap is not an engineering nuisance — it reflects deep physics. Domain randomization and real-data fine-tuning make the gap more manageable, but don't eliminate it. For any robotics deployment in an uncontrolled real-world environment, assume the system will need real-world data collection, real-world testing, and ongoing monitoring — not just simulation training. The physical world doesn't run in a container.
3. The current deployment frontier is structured environments — general-purpose robots remain a medium-term projection, not a 2026 reality. For AI to be useful in robotics right now, the most reliable path is constrained, well-defined tasks in controlled environments with engineered fixtures and limited variability — not general-purpose manipulation in arbitrary real-world settings. The technology is improving rapidly; calibrate your timelines to evidence, not to headlines from demo videos.
          
        
TL;DR
Robotics is the hardest domain in this arc because it requires not just prediction but physical action, in an open-ended world that simulation never fully captures.
Foundation-model robotics (RT-2, π0) extends multimodal vision-language models (Issue #20) to predict robot actions as a third modality — letting pretrained world knowledge and visual understanding directly inform physical control. This is a genuine architectural breakthrough over prior task-specific robotics approaches.
The sim-to-real gap — the systematic difference between simulated physics and real physics — remains a core technical challenge, addressed partially by domain randomization and real-data fine-tuning, but not eliminated by them.
The data problem: robot demonstration data is expensive and slow to collect — orders of magnitude smaller than the text and image datasets that powered language and vision foundation models.
What's working: structured industrial environments (long-solved), warehouse automation, foundation-model dexterous manipulation in lab settings. What remains open: general-purpose manipulation in uncontrolled real-world environments.
The verifiable-correctness pattern partially applies to specific tasks but doesn't drive broad generalization — verification of a successful grasp in one configuration doesn't transfer to a different configuration the way DFT verification transfers across chemical structures.
The physical world doesn't run in a container. That, more than any single technical limitation, is why general embodied AI remains an open problem.

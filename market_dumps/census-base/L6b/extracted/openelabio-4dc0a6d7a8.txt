Choosing LeRobot hardware is not simply a matter of comparing payload, motor torque, degrees of freedom or price.
SO-ARM101, Koch v1.1, OpenArm 2, LeKiwi and XLeRobot represent fundamentally different robot configurations. Some are fixed tabletop arms designed for straightforward imitation-learning experiments. Others combine manipulation with mobility, dual-arm coordination or research-grade contact capabilities.
The right question is therefore not:
Which robot has the best specifications?
It is:
Which platform most closely reproduces the physical structure of the tasks you eventually want to demonstrate, train and deploy?
A low-cost stationary arm may be the best choice for learning the complete LeRobot workflow. A mobile dual-arm robot may appear more capable, but it also introduces navigation, camera synchronization, base-position uncertainty, self-collision and much more complicated data requirements. A research-grade platform such as OpenArm may cost substantially more, yet its backdrivable actuators, higher payload and contact-rich manipulation capabilities can make it the more appropriate tool for advanced laboratory work.
This guide compares the five platforms by robot form factor, Leader–Follower configuration, actuator architecture, data-collection difficulty, software complexity, expansion potential and total cost of ownership.
Quick Answer: Which LeRobot Robot Should You Buy?
Choose SO-ARM101 if you want the lowest-cost and most accessible introduction to LeRobot, teleoperation and stationary imitation learning.
Choose Koch v1.1 if you want a more robust stationary Leader–Follower system built around the Dynamixel ecosystem.
Choose LeKiwi if the robot needs to move between locations but only requires one manipulation arm.
Choose XLeRobot if your project requires a low-cost mobile platform with two arms for household or whole-body embodied-AI experiments.
Choose OpenArm 2 if you need a research-grade bimanual platform with higher payload, seven degrees of freedom per arm, backdrivable joints, CAN-FD control and stronger contact-manipulation capabilities. OpenArm is designed and engineered by Enactic, while WowRobo manufactures and commercially delivers its hardware configurations.

Understanding Leader–Follower Systems Before You Buy
One of the most common purchasing mistakes is focusing only on the arm that performs the task.
For imitation learning, the Leader Arm or another teleoperation device can be just as important as the robot that executes the movement.
What Are Leader and Follower Arms?
A Leader Arm is manually moved by a human operator. Its joint positions are read by the control system and converted into commands for another robot.
A Follower Arm is the powered robot that reproduces those movements and physically interacts with the environment.
A typical LeRobot imitation-learning workflow is:
- 
The operator moves the Leader Arm.
- 
The Follower Arm reproduces the motion.
- 
Cameras record the task and environment.
- 
The system records joint states, actions and observations.
- 
A policy is trained from the demonstrations.
- 
The trained policy controls the Follower Arm autonomously.
LeRobot’s official real-world imitation-learning workflow supports teleoperation devices such as a Leader Arm or keyboard, followed by dataset recording, policy training and autonomous evaluation.
Why the Leader Arm Matters
The Leader affects more than basic robot control. It can influence:
- 
How naturally the operator demonstrates a task
- 
Whether the Leader and Follower joint structures match
- 
How much effort is required to move the Leader
- 
Teleoperation latency and smoothness
- 
The consistency of repeated demonstrations
- 
Operator fatigue during long data-collection sessions
- 
Whether the gripper input maps correctly to the Follower
- 
How much calibration is required before recording
SO-ARM101 illustrates this clearly. Its Follower uses six STS3215 motors with the same 1:345 gearing, while the Leader uses several different gear ratios so that it can support its own structure while remaining easier to manipulate manually.

The WowRobo TwinArm packing list states that its Koch-based package contains one Leader, one Follower, 12 Dynamixel servos, two driver boards, power components, cables and clamps. Buyers should still verify the selected product variant and current packing list before payment.
How Teleoperation Changes on Mobile Robots
For a stationary robot, the operator mainly controls the arm and gripper.
For a mobile manipulator, the operator may need to control:
- 
The robot base
- 
Driving direction
- 
Base orientation
- 
One or two arms
- 
One or two grippers
- 
Head or camera orientation
- 
Task transitions between navigation and manipulation
LeKiwi’s official LeRobot documentation describes a Follower arm installed on the mobile base and a separate Leader arm used for teleoperation. Depending on the build, the robot can run through a Raspberry Pi over SSH or through a wired host computer.
This means that “mobile LeRobot hardware” is not merely a stationary arm placed on wheels. It is a larger control, synchronization and data-engineering problem.

The Most Important Difference Is Robot Form Factor
Robot form factor determines what type of demonstrations can be collected and what physical problems the policy must solve.
Stationary Manipulation Platforms
SO-ARM101, Koch v1.1 and OpenArm are normally installed on a fixed desk, frame or laboratory structure.
This configuration is suitable for:
- 
Pick-and-place
- 
Sorting
- 
Object insertion
- 
Tabletop organization
- 
Tool interaction
- 
Cloth manipulation
- 
Bimanual coordination
- 
Repeatable vision-based experiments
- 
Standardized evaluation environments
A fixed base removes several sources of variation. The robot begins each episode from a known location, the cameras can remain in consistent positions, and the relationship between the arm and workspace is easier to reproduce.
That does not make stationary manipulation easy. Contact, occlusion, object variation and demonstration quality remain difficult problems. However, it reduces the number of variables that must be represented in the dataset.
Mobile Manipulation Platforms
LeKiwi and XLeRobot combine robot arms with mobile bases.
This enables tasks such as:
- 
Moving between tables
- 
Fetching objects from another area
- 
Transporting lightweight items
- 
Interacting with multiple workstations
- 
Household robotics demonstrations
- 
Navigation followed by manipulation
- 
Long-horizon embodied-AI tasks
Mobility also adds uncertainty:
- 
Wheel slip
- 
Base-position error
- 
Different approach angles
- 
Changing camera viewpoints
- 
Floor irregularities
- 
Battery state
- 
Wireless communication quality
- 
Localization drift
- 
Collision risks
- 
Navigation failures
LeKiwi is an open-source mobile manipulator associated with the LeRobot ecosystem and uses an omnidirectional base architecture. WowRobo currently offers versions with and without a Raspberry Pi and storage card, demonstrating why buyers must check the selected configuration rather than assuming that onboard computing is included.
Single-Arm vs Dual-Arm Tasks
A single arm is normally sufficient for:
- 
Picking up an object
- 
Placing an object in a container
- 
Pressing a button
- 
Moving a lightweight item
- 
Pushing or pulling a simple object
- 
Basic drawer interaction
- 
Simple tool movement
Two arms become valuable when the task requires:
- 
Holding an object with one hand while manipulating it with the other
- 
Folding cloth
- 
Opening containers
- 
Regrasping
- 
Coordinated lifting
- 
Handing objects between arms
- 
Stabilizing tools or workpieces
- 
Human-style household manipulation
However, two arms do not automatically improve performance. They increase the action dimension, calibration burden, collision risk and number of demonstrations required to cover meaningful behaviors.
Data-Collection Complexity by Robot Type
Hardware buyers frequently underestimate how strongly robot form factor affects dataset cost.
Stationary Single-Arm Data Collection
SO-ARM101 and Koch v1.1 have the simplest problem structure among the five platforms.
The robot base remains fixed, the number of controlled joints is relatively limited, and a single task can often be framed within a small workspace. External cameras can also be rigidly mounted.
This generally provides:
- 
More repeatable initial conditions
- 
Shorter episodes
- 
Fewer collision combinations
- 
Easier camera calibration
- 
Simpler failure analysis
- 
Lower demonstration requirements
- 
Faster iteration during early experiments
This is one reason SO-ARM101 is a strong entry point. It does not merely cost less; it reduces the number of simultaneous engineering problems the user must solve.
Stationary Dual-Arm Data Collection
OpenArm increases the manipulation state and action space because it has two seven-degree-of-freedom arms.
A bimanual dataset may need to preserve:
- 
Synchronization between the left and right arms
- 
Relative hand poses
- 
Gripper timing
- 
Object transfer events
- 
Arm-to-arm collision margins
- 
Contact states
- 
Wrist and scene-camera observations
OpenArm remains stationary by default, so base localization is not part of the normal task. This helps preserve repeatability even when the manipulation itself is complex.
Mobile Single-Arm Data Collection
LeKiwi adds base motion to the arm trajectory.
A complete episode may include:
- 
Driving toward a target
- 
Aligning the base
- 
Positioning the arm
- 
Manipulating the object
- 
Retracting the arm
- 
Driving to another destination
The dataset may therefore need to capture both navigation and manipulation phases. Base-position errors can also change the arm’s effective relationship to the object even when the arm follows the same joint trajectory.
Mobile Dual-Arm Data Collection
XLeRobot combines the largest number of variables:
- 
Mobile-base motion
- 
Two arms
- 
Two grippers
- 
Multiple cameras
- 
Whole-body geometry
- 
Longer task sequences
- 
More self-collision possibilities
- 
More failure-recovery situations
XLeRobot is built on projects including LeRobot, SO-100/SO-101 and LeKiwi. Its official repository presents it as a practical low-cost mobile home robot and continues to add hardware, control and simulation updates.
Moving from SO-ARM101 to XLeRobot is therefore not equivalent to purchasing one additional arm and a mobile base. It significantly increases the amount of data, calibration and policy engineering required.
SO-ARM101: Best Entry-Level LeRobot Hardware
What Is SO-ARM101?
SO-ARM101 is a low-cost open-source Leader–Follower robot-arm platform supported by LeRobot.
A typical setup contains:
- 
One manually controlled Leader Arm
- 
One powered Follower Arm
- 
Feetech bus servos
- 
MotorBus control hardware
- 
A power supply
- 
One or more cameras
- 
A host computer running LeRobot
The official LeRobot SO-101 documentation describes a six-motor Follower and a six-motor Leader. The Follower uses STS3215 motors with 1:345 gearing, while the Leader uses a combination of gear ratios intended to balance structural support and manual movement.
WowRobo offers multiple SO-ARM101 package levels, including printed parts with servos, an unassembled Leader–Follower kit with a camera, and an assembled Leader–Follower configuration with a camera. Current availability and contents should always be confirmed on the selected listing.
Why SO-ARM101 Is the Easiest Starting Point
SO-ARM101 concentrates the project around one clear manipulation problem.
The operator moves the Leader, the Follower reproduces the motion, cameras record the observation, and LeRobot records the action and state data.
Its advantages for first-time users include:
- 
A compact tabletop workspace
- 
A direct Leader–Follower relationship
- 
Relatively inexpensive actuators
- 
Lower replacement-part cost
- 
No mobile-base localization
- 
No dual-arm coordination
- 
A widely documented LeRobot workflow
- 
A manageable calibration process
The official LeRobot documentation includes port detection, motor setup and separate Leader and Follower calibration procedures. Calibration aligns the physical joint positions with their recorded values and is essential for consistent teleoperation and policy deployment.
Key Strengths
SO-ARM101 is particularly attractive because it offers:
- 
The lowest entry cost of the five platforms

Direct compatibility with common LeRobot workflows

Affordable experimentation

Compact dimensions

- 
A straightforward path from teleoperation to autonomous inference
A team can also use SO-ARM101 to determine whether its task, camera arrangement and policy architecture are viable before committing to more expensive hardware.

Koch v1.1: Best for a More Robust Leader–Follower Setup
What Is Koch v1.1?
Koch v1.1 is another Leader–Follower arm architecture supported by LeRobot.
The official LeRobot integration provides separate setup and calibration types for the Koch Leader and Koch Follower. Its calibration procedure follows the same general middle-position and full-range workflow used by SO-series arms.
WowRobo’s TwinArm product is based on the open-source Koch v1.1 design rather than being an unrelated proprietary arm. The manufacturer highlights production-oriented improvements such as a mixed-material gripper, standardized quality control and more durable engineering-plastic structures.
Koch v1.1 vs SO-ARM101
The central difference is not simply appearance.
SO-ARM101 is designed around lower-cost Feetech bus servos. Koch-style implementations generally use Dynamixel servos.
That difference affects:
- 
Component cost
- 
Diagnostic tools
- 
Motor configuration
- 
Software ecosystem
- 
Replacement-part availability
- 
Feedback quality
- 
Maintenance procedures
- 
Long-term ownership cost
Dynamixel is widely used in education and robotics research, providing a mature set of configuration and diagnostic tools. However, individual servos and replacement components typically cost more than comparable low-cost bus-servo parts.
Key Strengths
Koch v1.1 offers:
- 
A more premium servo ecosystem
- 
A direct Leader–Follower configuration
- 
Better suitability for repeated teleoperation
- 
Strong educational and research value
- 
A useful middle position between SO-ARM101 and OpenArm
- 
Easier adoption for laboratories already using Dynamixel hardware
WowRobo’s current packing list for its Koch-based TwinArm includes one Leader, one Follower, 12 Dynamixel servos, two servo driver boards, power components, cables and clamps.
Main Limitations

It is more expensive than SO-ARM101.

OpenArm 2: Best Research-Grade Bimanual Platform
What Is OpenArm?
OpenArm is an open-source seven-degree-of-freedom humanoid-arm platform developed for physical-AI research and contact-rich environments.
OpenArm 2 provides seven degrees of freedom per arm, QDD backdrivable joints, mechanical limits, a CAN-FD control bus, a metal structure and a compact parallel gripper with an in-hand camera. Its official published payload figures are 4.1 kg nominal and 6.0 kg peak under specifically defined test conditions.
Commercial attribution must remain clear:
- 
Enactic designs and engineers OpenArm.
- 
WowRobo manufactures and delivers commercial configurations.
- 
The hardware and software are open source.
- 
OpenArm should not be described as a project originally designed by WowRobo.

OpenArm’s Advantage in Contact-Rich Tasks
Many low-cost arms perform best when moving through free space from one pose to another.
Contact-rich manipulation requires the robot to interact continuously with objects, surfaces or tools. Examples include:

Backdrivable QDD joints and higher-quality real-time control make OpenArm better suited to this class of research than lightweight hobby-servo arms. This does not mean that every OpenArm task automatically includes force control, but the mechanical and control architecture provides a stronger foundation for compliant and contact-aware behavior.

LeKiwi: Best Low-Cost Mobile Manipulator
What Is LeKiwi?
LeKiwi is a low-cost open-source mobile manipulator that combines a LeRobot-compatible arm with a mobile base.
The project provides hardware-design, assembly and LeRobot setup resources. The official LeRobot documentation describes assembling one arm for the mobile base and another arm for teleoperation.
WowRobo’s current LeKiwi listing describes an omnidirectional drive platform and offers package options with or without a Raspberry Pi and 64 GB card.

In a stationary setup, the relationship between the arm and table remains fixed. In LeKiwi, a small change in the base position changes the arm’s reachable workspace relative to the object.

XLeRobot vs OpenArm
This comparison should not be reduced to “which robot is more advanced?”
Form Factor
XLeRobot is a lightweight mobile dual-arm platform.
OpenArm is a fixed research-grade dual-arm platform by default.
Manipulation Quality
XLeRobot prioritizes affordability, mobility and task variety.
OpenArm prioritizes actuator quality, payload, backdrivability, control frequency and contact interaction.
Deployment Complexity
XLeRobot must coordinate navigation, two arms and multiple viewpoints.
OpenArm has more advanced arm-level control requirements but benefits from a fixed base and more repeatable laboratory environment.
Scalability
XLeRobot provides an integrated mobile form factor without requiring the user to design a mobile robot from the ground up.
OpenArm offers stronger long-term potential for research-grade grippers, tactile sensors, vision systems and custom mobile-base integration. However, mounting OpenArm on an AMR requires significant mechanical, electrical and software engineering.
Choose XLeRobot to obtain a low-cost mobile dual-arm platform quickly.
Choose OpenArm to build a higher-performance and more extensible manipulation research system.
Hardware Quality and Actuator Differences
Feetech Bus Servos
Feetech servos are central to SO-ARM101 and related low-cost LeRobot hardware.
Their advantages include:
- 
Low cost
- 
Compact dimensions
- 
Accessible replacement parts
- 
Serial-bus control
- 
Suitability for education
- 
Support for lightweight robot structures
Their limitations include:
- 
Backlash
- 
Limited rigidity
- 
Heat under sustained load
- 
Lower payload
- 
Less suitable force behavior
- 
Sensitivity to correct model and voltage selection
SO-ARM101’s Leader and Follower do not necessarily use identical motor gear ratios, so replacement motors must be matched to the correct joint and role.
Dynamixel Servos
Dynamixel servos are used by Koch-style systems.
Their advantages include:
- 
Mature software tools
- 
Detailed configuration
- 
Broad educational adoption
- 
Useful status feedback
- 
Established robotics integration
Their disadvantages include:
- 
Higher unit cost
- 
More expensive spares
- 
Greater total kit cost
- 
Continued limitations relative to QDD research actuators
QDD Actuators
OpenArm uses quasi-direct-drive, backdrivable joints.
Their advantages include:
- 
Lower mechanical impedance
- 
Better manual interaction
- 
Stronger suitability for compliant control
- 
High torque
- 
Higher payload
- 
High-frequency CAN-based control
- 
Better foundation for contact-aware research
Their disadvantages include:
- 
Higher purchase cost
- 
More demanding power requirements
- 
More complicated CAN configuration
- 
Greater safety responsibility
- 
More expensive repair and replacement

Why the Cheapest Robot May Not Produce the Cheapest Project
A low-cost robot can become expensive when:
- 
The task exceeds its payload.
- 
The arm cannot reach the required workspace.
- 
Servo backlash produces inconsistent demonstrations.
- 
The project actually requires mobility.
- 
The task requires two arms.
- 
Contact causes repeated failures.
- 
The team spends months compensating for a hardware limitation.
Conversely, an expensive platform is not automatically economical. Purchasing OpenArm for simple tabletop sorting would add cost and complexity without necessarily improving the outcome.
The most economical platform is the least complex system that still reproduces the essential physical requirements of the target task.

Frequently Asked Questions
What Is the Best LeRobot Arm for Beginners?
SO-ARM101 is usually the best starting point because it combines low cost, a direct Leader–Follower workflow and relatively low data-collection complexity.
Do I Need a Leader Arm for LeRobot?
Not always. LeRobot can support other teleoperation devices, including keyboards and game controllers in some workflows. However, a matching Leader Arm is often the most direct method for collecting natural joint-space demonstrations.
Does Every SO-ARM101 Kit Include a Leader Arm?
No. Package contents vary. Some kits may include only parts and servos, while other packages include one Leader, one Follower and a camera. Verify the selected configuration.
Is Koch v1.1 Better Than SO-ARM101?
Koch v1.1 generally offers a more premium servo ecosystem and may be more suitable for repeated laboratory use. SO-ARM101 remains more affordable and may be the better choice for beginners or multi-unit deployment.
Better hardware does not guarantee a better trained policy. Dataset quality, camera placement, task design and demonstration consistency remain critical.
Is OpenArm Compatible With LeRobot?
LeRobot currently documents an OpenArm integration, including an OpenArm Leader teleoperation type and CAN interface. Compatibility should still be verified against the current OpenArm hardware and LeRobot software versions before deployment.
Is LeKiwi a Complete Robot?
That depends on the package. WowRobo currently offers versions with and without a Raspberry Pi, while the full project also requires an arm, mobile base, power, cameras and teleoperation hardware.
Does XLeRobot Include the Mobile Base?
Not every package does. WowRobo currently separates the dual-arm robot, base expansion and combined package.
Can SO-ARM101 Perform Household Tasks?
It can demonstrate small tabletop household tasks, such as moving lightweight objects or placing items into containers. Its fixed base, limited reach and limited payload prevent it from acting as a general household robot.
Is OpenArm Suitable for Beginners?
Usually not as a first robot. It is more appropriate for teams with a defined research objective, sufficient budget, safe workspace and experience with robotics software and electrical integration.
Which Platform Is Easiest for Policy Training?
A typical order from lower to higher system complexity is:
- 
SO-ARM101
- 
Koch v1.1
- 
OpenArm 2
- 
LeKiwi
- 
XLeRobot
This is not a universal performance ranking. It reflects the increasing action space, number of arms, base mobility and observation complexity.
Which Platform Has the Best Long-Term Scalability?
- 
SO-ARM101: best for scaling the number of low-cost stations
- 
Koch v1.1: best for maintaining a more robust tabletop teleoperation setup
- 
OpenArm 2: best for research-grade sensor, end-effector and platform expansion
- 
LeKiwi: best for progressing into affordable mobile manipulation
- 
XLeRobot: best for expanding into household and whole-body tasks
